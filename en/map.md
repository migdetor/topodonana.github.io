# The Map
<!--In a time when the digital has replaced the analogue, **this map is**, deliberately, **an anachronism**.
-->
Maps reflect our view of the world at a given moment. The moment Doñana is living through is, like its own ecology, shifting: ponds that dry out, centuries-old cork oaks that succumb, a marsh whose flooding becomes erratic, and—alongside a generational change—the fading of a singular way of inhabiting the land, born as an adaptation to a harsh environment.

That culture is written into the landscape; thousands of names fill a space that would otherwise appear wild. They tell stories of the territory, people, uses, and events –names that describe, guide, and warn. But who will remember them when those who spoke them are no longer here? Who will remember the most remote ponds when scrub erases them?

## From ponds to Doñana
This project began with a simple idea: putting the ponds on a map. But Doñana demands more. When you represent a pond, they differ in their <abbr title="Duration of flooding.">hydroperiod</abbr>, they may or may not have a <abbr title="A small artificial excavation made in the basin of a temporary pond so that groundwater can surface permanently and serve as a drinking trough for wild and domestic fauna.">zacallón</abbr>, centuries-old cork oaks, or connections to other ponds. The map grew out of coherence, because Doñana cannot be understood in isolation.

<figure style="margin: 0 0 1rem 0;">
  <img src="{{ "/assets/img/mapmarsh_red.jpg" | relative_url }}" alt="Topo Doñana" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.78em; opacity: 0.85; margin-top: 0.35rem; text-align: justify; text-justify: inter-word;">
Partial view of the Updated Map of Doñana National Park. 
  </figcaption>
</figure>

In this way of reading the territory, the human traces that structure it also appear: paths and firebreaks, estates and fences, rangers’ houses (many now demolished), watchtowers, palaces, wells, geodetic vertices, or hydrometeorological stations. All this information—together with other reference layers such as the National Park boundaries, elevations, slopes, and vegetation—has been brought together for the making of the map. 

It is worth distinguishing, though, between what has been taken as a **reference base** and what constitutes the project’s **own work**. The base layers—elevations, slopes, and other general cartography—come from institutional sources and have been incorporated **as they were published**, my work being to **adapt them to Doñana**: clipping them to the Park’s extent, harmonizing them so they fit together, making them operational within the map, and so on.

<figure style="margin: 0 0 1rem 0;">
  <img src="{{ "/assets/img/alcorn_red.jpg" | relative_url }}" alt="Topo Doñana" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.78em; opacity: 0.85; margin-top: 0.35rem; text-align: justify; text-justify: inter-word;">
Manual digitization of cork oaks through photointerpretation of orthophotographs. Photograph: Miguel de Felipe

But the map is not a compilation. Most of what makes it specific has had to be **collected, reconstructed, or created**. Some layers existed only in part—outdated, scattered across reports, old maps, or databases that were hard to use; others did not exist as such and have been produced *ex profeso*, through **photointerpretation**, checks, accumulated knowledge, and **many conversations with people from the area**: rangers, locals, researchers, and those who still name and walk the territory.

Then came the less visible work: **editing**, **correcting**, **standardizing**. Bringing order to names, codes, geometries, and attributes so that everything can be read in a single language.

<figure style="margin: 0 0 1rem 0;">
  <img src="{{ "/assets/img/mapprocess_red.jpg" | relative_url }}" alt="Topo Doñana" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.78em; opacity: 0.85; margin-top: 0.35rem; text-align: justify; text-justify: inter-word;">
Toponym review. Photograph: Miguel de Felipe
  </figcaption>
</figure>

And there is another distinction here: one thing is the **layers** (the data—downloadable and reusable), and another is the **map as a work**. The **cartographic composition**—the choice of what is included and what is left out, the visual hierarchy, the symbology, the labels, the balance between emptiness and detail, the way the whole reads on paper—is also part of the project, and it is **original and unpublished**. Ultimately, it is the author’s gaze placed on a sheet: a graphic synthesis that constitutes the map’s identity.

## A living project
Today it would be naïve to **publish a map** that only existed in print. That is why this project is presented in **two** complementary forms: **physical and digital**. The first preserves the value of the tangible as an object of consultation and synthesis, while the second functions as an **[open repository](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18109683)** where the information can be consulted, downloaded, and updated. 

<figure style="margin: 0 0 1rem 0;">
  <img src="{{ "/assets/img/dunasmap_red.jpg" | relative_url }}" alt="Topo Doñana" style="max-width: 100%; height: auto;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.78em; opacity: 0.85; margin-top: 0.35rem; text-align: justify; text-justify: inter-word;">
Partial view of the Updated Map of Doñana National Park. 
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The idea is for the map not to be a closed product, but a useful starting point for research, management, outreach, and learning. In that sense, this map is conceived as a **living project**: the open nature of the data allows the content to improve over time through **suggestions, corrections, and updates** from those who work in and walk Doñana—toponym locations, changes in infrastructure, revisions of names and boundaries, or any improvement that increases the usefulness of the whole.

That is why the map is published in versions; each update is recorded and traceable, so that while the physical map remains a fixed reference in time, its “digital life” can continue.
