# A jewel at risk
Only a handful of deltas, —due to their strategic position between the temperate and tropical zones of the world, concentrate such an extraordinary biodiversity that, more than 60 years ago, moved the international community to protect what we curently know as *Doñana*.

Over the last century, around 70% of the planet’s wetlands have disappeared, and it is estimated that a further 78% will do so by 2100. Doñana, despite being the most important wetland in Europe, has the dubious distinction of perfectly exemplifying the main challenges and threats faced by wetlands worldwide: agricultural intensification, increasing water demand and climate change.

# Doñana
Historically, *Doñana* was the name given to the territory between Coto del Rey (east of the village of El Rocío) and Marismillas (in front of Sanlúcar de Barrameda, by the other side of the Guadalquivir River). Today, Doñana is used in a broad sense to refer to the Doñana *Biosphere Reserve*: an extense area of ~265,000 hectares spanning the provinces of Cádiz, Huelva, and Seville. For the creation of **this map**, we have chosen to use Doñana to refer to the 54,000 ha of land under the protection status of the **National Park**, located between the Guadalquivir River and the Atlantic Ocean. 

This is a landscape tightly linked to water where, ponds, streams, and channels spread thorough sand dunes, woodlands, and Mediterranean scrublands on their way to the marsh. The climate here is Mediterranean, with mild winters (10.89 °C on average ± 1.71 SD) and warm summers (23.53 °C ± 2.06 Standard Deviation), and a marked seasonality in rainfall. Mean annual precipitation is around 568.2 mm (± 199 SD; for the period 1985–2018), of which ~80% typically falls in autumn and winter. Rainfall also shows strong interannual variability, ranging from 169 mm in the 2004–2005 hydrological year to 1027 mm in 1995–1996.

**Geomorphologically**, Doñana is the result of the evolution of an ancient estuary—the *Lacus Ligustinus*—progressively closed by a coastal sand barrier and filled by riverine and marine sediments. More recent aeolian dynamics dominated on this base, keeping a coastal dune system in motion. Three major geomorphological units can therefore be distinguished: the **Marshes** (on silts and clays), the **Cotos** or stabilized sands, and the coastal belt of **mobile dunes**. 

At present, Doñana should be understood as the product of the **interaction** between **surface water** and groundwater: beneath the protected area lies a large aquifer—approximately five times larger than the National Park itself—recharged by rainfall. While it behaves as a confined system under the marsh, in the sands it functions as an unconfined **aquifer** with a shallow water table, where gradients in water availability determine the dominant ecosystems across the Park.

# Its ecosystems
On this physical foundation, a highly dynamic mosaic of landscapes is organized: 
- The **marsh**—fed by a network of rivers, streams, channels, and by precipitation—functions as a vast floodplain (~30,000 ha) whose microtopography generates a diversity of habitats (*lucios, caños, quebradas, vetas, toruños, etc.*) that are fundamental for the great diversity and abundance of plants and animals found here. Particularly striking are the *hundreds of thousands of waterbirds* that use Doñana each year as a breeding site, a wintering area, or a stepping stone for transcontinental migrations. <!--However, since the 1950s, the marsh has become increasingly dependent on rainfall (which is highly variable) as a consequence of polderization and agricultural conversion around the marsh, which have diverted and overexploited the inflowing streams and rivers. -->

In parallel, the **sand and dune system** supports different communities *linked to the depth of the water table*:
- In higher areas—farther from the water table—the **monte blanco** appears, dominated by xerophilous vegetation such as jaguarzo (*Cistus halimifolius*), junipers (*Juniperus turbinata*), and aromatic plants like rosemary (*Salvia rosmarinus*), almoradux (*Thymus matischina*), or Spanish lavender (*Lavandula stoechas*).
- In more depressed areas, the shallow water table sustains a diverse community of phreatophytic vegetation—the **monte negro**—represented by cork oaks (*Quercus suber*), heathlands (*Erica scoparia, E. ciliaris, E. umbellata, Calluna vulgaris*), brambles (*Rubus ulmifolius*), and mock privet (*Phyllirea angustifolia*).
- In the **corrales**—interdunal valleys—the stone pine woodland (*Pinus pinea*) gives way to humid intradunal depressions where the influence of the water table is constant.
- But it is in the even more depressed zones where direct aquifer discharge occurs, creating a network of more than 3,000 **ponds**; most of them temporary.

Together, this diversity of ecosystems supports an exceptional biodiversity of more than **1,800 animal and plant species**. This is precisely because small differences in the terrain—often imperceptible to the naked eye—produce ecologically meaningful changes in water availability, which in turn generate a great diversity of habitats.

But this same *dependence on water* makes Doñana a highly sensitive system to hydrological change. Recent evidence shows that the shortening of hydroperiods and the drying of ponds—linked to modifications of the drainage network, **overexploitation of the aquifer**, and exacerbated by periods of low rainfall—has translated into a measurable **loss** of **biodiversity** at the scale of the National Park. And these changes do not stop here: the conditions experienced by wintering waterbirds in Doñana reverberate through their breeding populations across Europe.
